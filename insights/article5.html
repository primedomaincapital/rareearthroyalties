How a Rare Earth Royalty Is Actually Calculated: NSR, the Stream, and the Fine Print

A 2 percent net smelter return sounds small and precise. It is neither. The percentage is the least important term in the agreement — the price definition and the deduction schedule decide what the royalty is actually worth.

By Prime Domain Capital ResearchNovember 19, 20267-minute read

A royalty buyer in Denver in 2026 is offered two percent of a rare-earth mine's net smelter return. The number sounds small and precise. It is neither. The percentage is the least important term in the agreement, and the buyer who negotiates the percentage while ignoring the definitions has bought a number, not an income stream. The net smelter return is a formula with four moving parts, and in rare earths every one of them moves more than it does in gold.

01 — The formula, and what it leaves out

The formula itself is simple. The net smelter return is the gross revenue from the metal sold, less the allowable off-site deductions. The royalty payment is the royalty rate multiplied by that net figure. The allowable deductions are set out in the royalty agreement, and they typically include smelting and refining charges, penalties for impurities in the concentrate, transport from the mine gate to the smelter or refinery, insurance and security in transit, and sometimes marketing or sampling costs. What is not deductible is the point that makes the royalty valuable: the costs incurred at the mine — mining, milling, labour, energy, sustaining capital — are never deducted. The royalty holder does not pay for the hole in the ground. Royalty rates on precious metals usually run from about 0.5 percent to 5 percent, and many agreements add sliding scales that change the rate with the metal price, or buyback clauses that let the operator repurchase part of the royalty, often half, for a fixed payment.

Take an illustrative example, worked at the numbers the market actually trades. A mine sells 500 tonnes of neodymium-praseodymium oxide in a year at $110 a kilogram — the Pentagon's price floor for MP Materials and Lynas — for gross revenue of $55 million. Off-site deductions run $6 a kilogram for transport, separation tolling, and insurance, or $3 million. The net smelter return is $52 million. A 2 percent royalty pays $1.04 million a year. If the price rises to $124 a kilogram, the 2022 peak, the royalty rises to about $1.19 million with no extra cost to the holder, even if the mine's own costs have gone up. That is the royalty's appeal in one calculation: the payment floats with the price, and the costs do not touch it.

02 — The rare-earth twist: which price?

Gold is sold as doré at a transparent price, and a gold NSR is arithmetic. Rare earths are sold as oxides, concentrates, or metals, and the price depends on which one the contract names. Neodymium-praseodymium oxide averaged $92 a kilogram in 2021, spiked to $124 in 2022, fell to $75 in 2023 and $55 in 2024, and the U.S. Geological Survey estimates it recovered to roughly $69 in 2025 — a swing of more than 2.2 times from peak to trough in under three years. The Pentagon's floor is $110 a kilogram. Benchmark put oxide at $95 a kilogram delivered into Europe and North America around August 20, 2026, below the $110 floor. The same kilogram, three prices, and the royalty's value depends entirely on which one the agreement defines as the price. A royalty written against the Chinese domestic price is a different asset from a royalty written against the ex-China assessment, and the contract rarely says so in plain language.

The basket makes it harder still, because a rare-earth deposit is never one metal. The heavy rare earths carry the value in small quantities: terbium oxide averaged about $1,010 a kilogram in 2025 on the Geological Survey's figures, down from $2,050 in 2022 and $812 in 2024, while dysprosium averaged $239 in 2025, down from $410 in 2021. Neodymium and praseodymium, the volume metals, averaged $73 and $74 a kilogram. The ex-China assessments run far higher than any of those averages: Fastmarkets assessed terbium oxide at $5,000 to $8,000 a kilogram on October 1, 2026, and dysprosium at $2,500 to $3,500, while Argus data put ex-China terbium near $4,500 and dysprosium near $1,450 in May 2026. A deposit's payable basket is a weighted average of all of these, and the weighting — which elements count as payable, at what recovery, at what price — is negotiated, not standard. Two royalties on the same mine can pay different amounts, and both can be correct.

03 — What a real deposit's numbers look like

American Rare Earths' Cowboy State Mine in Wyoming gives a current picture of what the formula meets in practice. Its prefeasibility study, published November 19, 2026, puts the after-tax net present value at about US$1.3 billion at an 8 percent discount rate, with an internal rate of return of 24 percent and pre-production capital of about US$840 million for a 4.5-million-tonne-a-year open pit running 26 years. The maiden probable reserve is 116.4 million tonnes at 1,257 parts per million of payable rare earth oxide. The pit economics use a mining cost of US$5.38 a tonne, a process cost of US$28.28, and general and administrative costs of US$1.10, with a Wyoming state royalty of 2.50 percent, and the reserve is reported above a cut-off of about 875 parts per million. The state royalty is the number to watch: it is calculated on the same payable-oxide basis the NSR uses, and it is the only royalty in the project that is already fixed. A private royalty layered on top of it would take its percentage from the same net figure, after the same deductions, and the two would stack.

The newest development in royalty math is the price floor, which turns the price-definition problem into an advantage. On November 9, 2026, USA Rare Earth announced a 15-year, 100 percent take-or-pay offtake for its Serra Verde operation in Brazil, sold to a U.S. government-funded vehicle, with floors of $110 a kilogram for neodymium and praseodymium, $575 for dysprosium, and $2,050 for terbium — and the seller also paid 70 percent of the amount by which the non-China market price exceeds each floor, less a fixed separation cost. The company reports recent non-China prices running 14 to 16 times China prices for dysprosium oxide and 6 to 9 times for terbium. A royalty written against a floor-backed offtake is underwriting a contracted minimum, not a commodity price, and the 70-percent upside share means the royalty participates in the premium without taking the price risk of the uncontracted market.

04 — The honest caveat

There are good reasons not to treat a rare-earth NSR like a gold NSR with different metals in it. There is no liquid spot market, so the price in the formula is an assessment, and assessments for the same oxide can differ by a factor of five — the Geological Survey's $1,010 average for terbium against Fastmarkets' $5,000-to-$8,000 ex-China range is the same material, the same month, and a five-fold gap. The royalty holder is exposed to the operator's cost allocation, because what counts as an allowable deduction is negotiated in the agreement, not set by a standard, and a separation toll can be booked as an off-site deduction or as a processing cost depending on how the contract is written. And a royalty on oxide revenue captures none of the separation margin unless the contract says so — the value created between the concentrate and the oxide belongs to whoever owns the separation plant, and in most projects that is not the mine. The percentage is the least important term. The definitions are the deal.

The implication for anyone buying or selling a rare-earth royalty is to read the agreement in a specific order. Read the price-definition clause first: which oxide, which assessment, which delivery basis, and whether a floor-backed offtake sets the minimum. Read the deduction schedule second: what is allowable, what is capped, and whether separation is inside or outside the net figure. Read the percentage third. A 2 percent royalty on a $52 million net return is $1.04 million a year, and the difference between a good royalty and a bad one is not the 2. It is everything after it.
