The Magnet Maker's Margin: Who Captures the Value Between Oxide and Motor

A kilogram of neodymium-praseodymium oxide sells for about $110. The magnet in an electric motor sells for far more — and the margin does not sit where most investors think it does. Seven stages stand between the oxide and the motor, and the money pools in two of them.

By Prime Domain Capital ResearchNovember 19, 20267-minute read

The traction motor under the hood of a 2026 Mustang Mach-E carries between one and three kilograms of neodymium-iron-boron permanent magnet, and the dysprosium and terbium that keep that magnet from demagnetising under hard acceleration runs to roughly 50 to 200 grams. The whole assembly weighs less than a suitcase and costs a few hundred dollars. Behind it sits a supply chain of seven stages — mining, concentration, chemical separation, metal reduction, alloying, magnet manufacturing, and magnetisation — and a value distribution that most investors have exactly backwards. The magnet maker, the company whose name is on the finished product, earns the thinnest margin in the chain. The money pools upstream, in the two stages the West barely has.

01 — Where the margin actually sits

ABI Analytics' 2026 sizing of the chain puts the margins in plain numbers. Upstream mining earns 25 to 40 percent EBITDA on a small revenue base. Concentrate-to-separation earns 35 to 50 percent, and refining to oxides and metals earns 30 to 45 percent — the two fattest tiers in the chain, and the two where China holds roughly 70 percent and 80 percent of the capacity. Magnet manufacture earns 12 to 22 percent, on a revenue pool that reaches $66 billion by 2035, with 87 percent of it Chinese. Downstream carmakers and turbine builders earn 5 to 15 percent on the magnet content of their products. The industry is worth $33 billion in 2024, $40 billion in 2025, and $77 billion in 2030 on ABI's base case, reaching $118 billion by 2035 — an 11.4 percent compound annual growth rate — and cumulative capital spending from 2025 to 2035 totals $135 billion, split $48 billion to mining and concentrate, $50 billion to separation and refining, and $30 billion to magnet manufacturing. The capital follows the margin, and the margin sits in the middle of the chain.

The concentration is the story. China controls 69 percent of global rare-earth mining, approximately 90 percent of chemical separation, and 94 percent of permanent magnet manufacturing. The entire non-China world produces perhaps 20,000 to 25,000 tonnes of NdFeB magnets a year, and the United States had essentially zero large-scale magnet manufacturing until MP Materials began trial production in late 2025. Non-China separation capacity for neodymium-praseodymium is about 10,000 tonnes a year — Lynas' LAMP plant in Malaysia at about 7,000 tonnes, MP Materials at 2,599 tonnes in 2025 after doubling from 2024, Energy Fuels' White Mesa at about 1,000 tonnes, and Neo Performance in Estonia — against global NdPr demand of roughly 55,000 to 60,000 tonnes and growing. The magnet maker's margin is thin because the magnet maker is a price taker twice over: it buys oxide at a price set by the separator, and it sells magnets at a price set by the carmaker.

02 — The integration play

The companies that understand this are not building magnet plants. They are building chains. MP Materials reported third-quarter 2026 results on November 5: NdPr production of 1,018 metric tons, up 41 percent year over year, and NdPr sales of 1,020 tons, up 94 percent, with $8.1 million of price-protection income under its Pentagon agreement and revenue plus that income totalling $155.8 million. Its heavy rare-earth separation is set to begin commercial production in the fourth quarter of 2026, with saleable magnets shipping to General Motors in the same quarter and Apple deliveries starting in 2027 under a partnership worth more than $500 million, backed by a $40 million Apple prepayment. The Pentagon's July 2025 agreement set a ten-year, $110-a-kilogram floor on MP's NdPr output, took a 15 percent equity stake through a $400 million preferred investment, and extended a $150 million loan — and Washington committed to buy or place every magnet from the company's planned facility, due to start commissioning in 2028. MP's Project Swarm, launched in July 2026, lets drone makers reserve future magnet output. The strategy is not to make magnets. It is to own the separation margin and the magnet margin in the same building, with the government as the buyer of last resort.

Lynas Rare Earths is running the same play from the other side of the Pacific. On March 16, 2026, Lynas signed a binding letter of intent through its U.S. subsidiary to supply the Department of War with $96 million of rare-earth oxides over four years, with the same $110-a-kilogram NdPr floor the Pentagon gave MP. Its LAMP plant in Gebeng, Malaysia, has operated commercially since 2012, and samarium oxide production began there in March 2026, a month ahead of schedule, making Lynas the only producer of separated heavy rare earths outside China at scale. The two Pentagon deals — MP in July 2025, Lynas in March 2026 — are the same instrument applied twice: a price floor that converts the separation margin from a commodity gamble into a contracted return, and a government buyer that converts the magnet margin from a negotiation into an order.

03 — The honest caveat

Magnet makers' margins are thin for a reason, and the reason is not only market power. Magnet manufacturing is a scale and yield business: the value is in the sintering, the coating, and the defect rate, and a plant that runs at 60 percent yield loses money at any oxide price. The Chinese producers earned their 87 percent share over thirty years of learning-curve effects, state-subsidised infrastructure, and domestic cerium and lanthanum demand that absorbs the byproducts the West has no buyer for. A Western magnet plant that buys oxide at $110 a kilogram and sells magnets into a market where the Chinese incumbent's fully loaded NdPr cost is $25 to $40 a kilogram is not competing on margin. It is competing on a government contract, and government contracts can be slow, conditional, and political. The producers' own equities tell the same story: Canaccord's covered rare-earth producers are down 28 percent on average and developers 17 percent, even as non-China NdPr has risen 23 percent year to date — the physical market is firming and the equities are falling, because the market does not yet believe the margin will survive the build-out.

The demand, at least, is not in doubt. NdFeB magnet consumption is forecast to grow at a 7.3 percent compound annual rate to about 365,000 tonnes by 2035, with the global NdPr market in deficit through 2028 and roughly 7 percent short this year. Magnet output is forecast at 280,000 tonnes in 2025, 430,000 in 2030, and 580,000 in 2035, and the blended rare-earth-oxide basket value at the mine is forecast to rise from $5.80 a kilogram in 2025 to $9.30 in 2030 and $12.80 in 2035. Every electric vehicle needs its kilogram or three, every offshore turbine needs its tonnes, and the heavy rare earths that make those magnets survive heat are the scarcest part of the chain. The question is not whether the demand arrives. It is who captures the margin on the way from the oxide to the motor — and the answer, for now, is the separator, not the magnet maker.

That is the implication for a royalty holder, and it is a practical one. A royalty written on a mine's oxide revenue captures the mining margin and none of the separation margin, because the separation happens after the royalty is calculated. A royalty written on the separated oxide, or on a stream that follows the material through separation, captures the fattest tier in the chain. The difference between the two is not a drafting detail. It is the difference between a claim on $5.80-a-kilogram rock and a claim on a $110-a-kilogram floor — and the companies building the new Western chain, MP and Lynas both, are building it precisely so that the value stays in the building they own.
