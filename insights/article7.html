The Recycling Royalty: What Urban Mining Means for Rare Earth Owners

Less than 1 percent of rare earths are recycled today. The European Union wants a quarter of strategic material consumption to come from recycling by 2030. The urban mine is real — but the royalty on it has not been written yet.

By Prime Domain Capital ResearchNovember 19, 20267-minute read

In Mesa, Arizona, in September 2026, Cyclic Materials opened its first commercial-scale rare-earth magnet recycling facility, running a proprietary process that pulls magnets out of end-of-life products and turns them back into mixed rare-earth oxides. Four months earlier, in January, the same company had committed $82 million to a recycling park in McBee, South Carolina, designed to process 2,000 tonnes of magnet material a year and expand to 6,000, producing 600 tonnes of mixed oxides a year at first and 1,800 over time. The feedstock is not a mine. It is hard drives, motors, MRI machines, and electric vehicles — the urban mine, arriving on trucks instead of out of a pit. The question for a royalty holder is a simple one, and nobody has answered it yet: who owns the royalty on material that was already mined once?

01 — How small the starting point is

Global rare-earth recycling rates remain below 1 percent to date. That is the number to hold in mind through everything that follows, because it makes the rest of the story a story about a future, not a present. The rare-earth recycling market is worth $560 million in 2025 and is projected to reach $2.17 billion by 2035, a compound annual growth rate of 14.5 percent. Permanent magnets are the largest source at 38 percent of 2025 value, and they also grow fastest, at 17.1 percent. Direct magnet recycling — the short process that skips full chemical separation — grows at 19.9 percent a year against 10.5 percent for pyrometallurgical routes, because it preserves the alloy instead of dissolving it. The permanent magnet recycling services market, measured separately, was estimated at $1.42 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $4.18 billion by 2035, an 11.4 percent compound rate. The wider rare-earth elements market grew from $14.03 billion in 2025 to $16.25 billion in 2026 and is projected to reach $41.15 billion by 2034. Recycling is a rounding error inside a market that is itself small — and it is the fastest-growing rounding error in the chain.

The policy tailwind is real, and it has a date on it. The European Union's Critical Raw Materials Act mandates that at least 25 percent of strategic raw material consumption come from recycling by 2030. China's export controls in 2025 turned the urban mine from an environmental slogan into a supply-chain imperative, and the United States and Europe accelerated support for domestic recycling capacity through the same year. The first volumes, every forecaster agrees, will come from manufacturing scrap — the offcuts and rejects of magnet plants — with true end-of-life volumes from vehicles and turbines building later in the period as collection networks mature. An electric vehicle sold in 2024 does not reach a recycler until the 2040s. A wind turbine installed this year runs for twenty-five. The urban mine's ore body is being built now, and it will not be mined for a generation.

02 — The two routes, and who owns them

Two routes are being built in parallel in North America, and the difference between them is the difference between a royalty and a factory. MP Materials is pursuing the only full-chain closed loop in the West — mine, separation, recycling, magnet. On July 15, 2025, MP and Apple announced a $500 million collaboration to build a dedicated recycling line at Mountain Pass, California, processing waste magnets into feedstock for the Fort Worth, Texas, Independence magnet plant, delivering 100 percent recycled-content magnets starting in 2027. Apple prepaid $200 million, and Independence magnet capacity will expand from 1,000 tonnes a year to 3,000. The key to the approach is that the primary mining business cross-subsidises the recycling line, letting recycled material bypass Chinese oxide export licensing and be consumed directly by the magnet plant. One analyst estimate suggests the partnership could let the United States contribute about 20 percent of global recycled rare-earth production by 2030 — a forecast, not a company disclosure, and worth labelling as such.

Noveon Magnetics of San Marcos, Texas, is the other route: a pure recycling plant with no mine at all. Its Magnet-to-Magnet process selectively dismantles hard disk, motor, and MRI magnets without crushing the casing, and the company says its recycled powder can be blended 100 percent back into sintered NdFeB without downgrading the grade. In February 2025 it signed an offtake agreement with Nidec, one of the world's largest motor manufacturers, embedding the pure-recycling business model into the motor supply chain. Cyclic Materials uses a hub-and-spoke network to secure feedstock, collecting magnets in one place and processing them in another. HyProMag, Carester, Solvay, and Neo Performance Materials are building in the same direction in Europe. The competition is no longer about who can recover the most metal in a laboratory. It is about who can stably secure waste magnets, and who can turn recycled powder into material a magnet manufacturer will sign an offtake for.

The feedstock question decides which recyclers survive, and the answer is not the one the headlines suggest. The first volumes come from manufacturing scrap — the offcuts and rejects of magnet plants, concentrated in one place, uniform in composition, and available now — not from the electric vehicles and wind turbines that will not reach a recycler for twenty years. A recycler that secures a magnet plant's scrap stream has a feedstock contract, not a collection network, and the volumes are measured in tonnes a year, not tens of thousands. The end-of-life wave is real, but it is a 2040s story, and the recyclers being funded today have to survive on scrap until it arrives.

03 — The honest caveat

There are good reasons not to treat recycling as a near-term threat to mined supply, and the first is the feedstock clock. The vehicles and turbines that will supply the urban mine in the 2040s are still in service, and the collection networks that will gather them do not exist yet. The second is the grade problem: a recycled magnet is a mixture of alloys from different manufacturers, different years, and different specifications, and blending it back into a sintered magnet without downgrading the grade is a technical achievement, not a given — Noveon's claim is exactly that, a claim, and the offtake with Nidec is the evidence that a buyer believes it. The third is the royalty question itself, which is the one this article is about: a royalty on a mine pays on material the mine produces. Recycled material was not produced by the mine. It was produced by a motor, twenty years after the mine sold it. No existing royalty agreement covers it, and the recycler, the magnet maker, and the original mine's royalty holder all have a plausible claim to the value — which means, in practice, that none of them has one until a new agreement is written.

The implication is not that recycling destroys the royalty model. It is that the royalty model has to be rewritten for the second life of the material. A royalty holder whose agreement covers only mine production owns nothing in the recycling stream, and a recycler who buys waste magnets owns the feedstock but not necessarily the right to the separated oxides if the original sale carried a retained interest. The instruments that will matter are new: royalties on recycled output, written against the recycler rather than the miner; streams on magnet manufacturing scrap, where the feedstock is concentrated and the volumes arrive first; and offtake-linked payments, where the royalty follows the material into the magnet plant rather than stopping at the mine gate. The urban mine is real, the market is growing at 14.5 percent a year, and the European mandate gives it a deadline. The royalty on it has not been written yet — and the first party to write a good one will own a claim on a resource that never depletes, because every magnet ever made is still out there, waiting to come back.
